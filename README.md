# WRO 2026 Future Engineers

This project is our WRO Future Engineers robot, developed with the goal of completing the competition challenge efficiently, reliably, and with as few accidents as possible. Our main target is to complete three laps within three minutes while maintaining accurate movement and avoiding unnecessary collisions. The robot has been developed through testing and several changes to both the hardware and software. The final version uses a LEGO SPIKE Prime Hub as the main controller, LEGO components for the mechanical structure, SPIKE Prime motors and sensors, a HuskyLens camera, and an ESP32 with a servo to allow the HuskyLens to communicate information to the SPIKE Prime system.

The robot has a LEGO-based chassis with approximate dimensions of 23 × 11 × 27 cm. The chassis is designed with a hollow structure to reduce unnecessary weight. LEGO was selected as the main construction material because it is easy to use, lightweight, stable, and simple to modify during development. This was particularly useful because the robot had to be changed and tested several times while we worked on improving its movement and turning performance. The LEGO structure also allowed us to make adjustments without having to rebuild the entire robot from the beginning.

The robot uses four wheels in total. The wheels have an approximate diameter of two inches, or about 5.08 cm. The SPIKE Prime Hub is positioned around the middle of the robot. This central placement helps keep the main controller within the body of the robot rather than placing it far toward one side. The drive system uses one small SPIKE Prime motor to drive the front two wheels. The drive system uses direct drive without gears. The steering system uses one large SPIKE Prime motor. The front two wheels are connected by a LEGO axle or bar, and the large motor rotates this bar so that the front wheels change direction. This provides the steering movement required by the robot.

The mechanical design was developed with simplicity in mind. Instead of using a complicated gear system, the drive motor is connected directly to the drive mechanism. This makes the system easier to understand and modify. The steering mechanism was also built using LEGO components so that the front wheels could be turned together. One of the main mechanical challenges during development was turning. Getting the robot to turn in a useful and repeatable way required testing and adjustment. The current version turns fairly well compared with the earlier versions, although turning was one of the main areas that required attention during development.

The chassis has remained stable during testing. We did not observe major problems with the structure bending, becoming loose, or coming apart during normal operation. We also did not identify a major tipping or leaning problem during our testing. These characteristics were important because the robot needs to remain stable while moving and turning. A stable structure makes it easier for the sensors to remain in their intended positions and allows the robot to behave more consistently during runs.

One of the most important decisions in the development of the robot was changing the platform used to control it. Our first version was built around a Raspberry Pi 4. This version was difficult and time-consuming to develop because we had to spend a lot of time working on the system and getting it to behave as required. Pairing and communication were also difficult during this stage. Because of these problems, we decided to change to the SPIKE Prime system. The second major version of the robot was therefore built using LEGO and SPIKE Prime. This change made the system easier for us to use and allowed us to make progress much more quickly. The move from the Raspberry Pi 4 version to the SPIKE Prime version was one of the biggest improvements made during the project.

The electronics system is controlled mainly by the LEGO SPIKE Prime Hub. The robot uses distance sensors, a SPIKE Prime color sensor, and a HuskyLens camera. There are distance sensors positioned around the robot, including one at the front and sensors on the sides. The front distance sensor is used to detect the environment in front of the robot, while the side distance sensors provide information about the robot's surroundings. These sensors are important for detecting distances and helping the robot make movement decisions.

The HuskyLens is mounted at the front of the robot and faces forward. It is used to detect colors. The color information is important because the robot uses it as part of its decision-making process. We originally used the SPIKE Prime color sensor by itself, but later added the HuskyLens because we found it to be more accurate for the color detection that we needed. This was an improvement to the sensing system because it gave us another way to identify the required color information.

The HuskyLens cannot directly communicate all of its information to the SPIKE Prime Hub in the way required by our system. To solve this problem, we use an ESP32 and a servo as an interface between the HuskyLens and the SPIKE Prime color sensor. The system works through the chain of HuskyLens to ESP32 to servo to SPIKE Prime color sensor and then to the SPIKE Prime Hub. The HuskyLens detects the color, the ESP32 processes the relevant information, and the servo is used to communicate the color change to the SPIKE Prime color sensor. The SPIKE Prime system can then use the color sensor information as part of its control process.

The SPIKE Prime Hub is powered using its rechargeable battery. The motors and sensors are connected to the SPIKE Prime Hub using the LEGO connection system. We did not make custom power wiring for the robot, and the main system does not use a separate external power supply. This keeps the electronics relatively simple and makes the system easier to assemble and modify.

We have not measured exact electrical values such as current consumption, total power consumption, or other detailed electrical measurements. These values are therefore not included as measured specifications of the robot. If we perform these measurements later, they can be added to the documentation. For the current version, the important point is that the robot uses the SPIKE Prime rechargeable battery as its main power source and the SPIKE Prime Hub as its main controller.

The software for the robot is written in Python. The program controls the motors, reads information from the sensors, and controls the robot's movement. The basic behavior is that the robot drives forward, detects when it needs to turn, performs the turn, and continues moving. The program also counts turns. After twelve turns, the robot is programmed to continue for approximately 30 cm and then stop. This provides a defined stopping behavior for the current program.

The robot normally drives at a fixed speed during normal movement. When it approaches a turn, the speed is reduced to help with the turning process. The steering system uses a fixed turning angle. The exact numerical value of the steering angle has not been measured and is therefore not listed as a specific number in this documentation. The turning behavior was adjusted through testing so that the robot could turn more effectively.

The distance sensors are used to detect distances around the robot. The front distance sensor helps detect what is ahead, while the side sensors provide additional information about the environment. The HuskyLens and SPIKE Prime color sensor provide color information that is used together with the distance information when the robot makes decisions. The robot therefore does not depend on only one sensor for its complete behavior. Instead, information from the available sensors is used to determine what action should be taken.

The general decision process used by the robot is based on detecting a situation, checking the available color information, deciding whether the robot should turn right or left, and then steering the robot. In a simplified form, the process can be described as detecting the situation, checking the color, deciding on the direction, and steering. The exact numerical sensor thresholds used by the final program have not been recorded in this documentation, so they are not stated as specific values.

When an unexpected sensor reading occurs, the program can retry the detection rather than immediately continuing with an incorrect decision. This gives the robot another opportunity to receive a usable sensor reading. This behavior is useful because sensor readings can sometimes not provide the expected information on the first attempt.

The algorithm was selected because it is straightforward and easier for us to program and understand. Our main priority was to create a system that could reliably complete the course rather than making the software unnecessarily complicated. The algorithm was developed through testing and tuning. During development, we tested different detection lengths for the distance sensors and changed values such as sensor thresholds, motor speed, and the steering angle. These changes were made based on how the robot behaved during testing.

Testing was an important part of the development process. Rather than assuming that the first design would work correctly, we tested the robot and used the results to make changes. Turning was one of the main areas that required testing because the robot needed to change direction accurately while maintaining its position and avoiding accidents. The distance sensor detection behavior was also tested by trying different detection lengths. Motor speed and steering settings were adjusted to find behavior that worked better on the course.

The project has gone through two major versions. The first version was developed using a Raspberry Pi 4. This version was difficult to develop because of the amount of work required to make the system behave correctly and because pairing was difficult. The second version moved to LEGO and SPIKE Prime. This change made development easier and allowed us to make progress more quickly. The switch to LEGO and SPIKE Prime was therefore one of the most significant changes made during the project.

The current robot combines a simple LEGO mechanical structure with a SPIKE Prime control system and additional sensing through HuskyLens and ESP32. The mechanical design focuses on keeping the robot lightweight, stable, and easy to modify. The electronics focus on using the SPIKE Prime Hub, distance sensors, the SPIKE color sensor, and the HuskyLens to provide the information needed for the robot's movement decisions. The software connects these components together and controls the robot according to the programmed behavior.

The project is designed around the goal of completing three laps within three minutes while minimizing accidents. Speed is therefore important, but reliability is also important. A robot that moves quickly but frequently makes incorrect turns or has accidents would not meet the project's goal as effectively as a robot that maintains a good balance between movement speed and accuracy. For this reason, testing and tuning focused on the interaction between sensor detection, motor speed, and turning behavior.

There are some specifications that have not yet been measured. We have not recorded an exact top speed, exact motor torque, exact steering angle, exact total robot weight, or detailed electrical power consumption. These values should not be treated as known specifications. If more detailed measurements are taken in future testing, they can be added to the repository to make the technical documentation more complete.

The current design has several strengths. The LEGO chassis is lightweight and easy to modify. The structure has remained stable during testing without major bending or loosening problems. The SPIKE Prime Hub provides a simpler development platform than the Raspberry Pi 4 system that we originally tried. The combination of distance sensors, the SPIKE Prime color sensor, and HuskyLens provides multiple sources of information for the robot. The software is also straightforward, making it easier to understand, test, and adjust.

At the same time, turning remains an important area of the robot's development. Although the current robot turns fairly well, turning was one of the biggest mechanical and programming challenges during development. Further testing could be used to improve the consistency of turns and to determine more accurate mechanical measurements such as the steering angle. Additional testing could also provide measured speed, weight, power consumption, and other useful technical information.

The development process demonstrates an iterative approach. We began with one hardware and control approach, identified problems, and then changed the system. We tested the new system, adjusted sensor and motor settings, and continued improving the robot. Instead of treating the original design as final, we used the results from testing to guide changes. This process helped us move from the Raspberry Pi 4 version to the SPIKE Prime version and improve the robot's overall usability.

The final system is intended to be understandable and reproducible from the information provided in this repository. The mechanical design, electronics, software, algorithms, testing, iterations, photos, and videos can be organized in separate folders so that each part of the project can be documented clearly. The documentation focuses on the actual robot and its development rather than including unnecessary material that does not contribute to understanding the project.

Overall, this project represents the development of a WRO Future Engineers robot from an initial Raspberry Pi 4 concept to a LEGO SPIKE Prime-based system. The robot uses a lightweight LEGO chassis, four wheels, a small drive motor, a large steering motor, distance sensors, a SPIKE Prime color sensor, a HuskyLens camera, an ESP32, and a servo interface. Its software is written in Python and uses sensor information to control movement, turning, and stopping behavior. Through testing and iteration, the robot was adjusted to improve its turning, sensor detection, motor behavior, and overall reliability.

The main lesson from the development process was the importance of testing and making changes based on actual robot behavior. The switch from Raspberry Pi 4 to SPIKE Prime significantly improved the development process for our team, while the addition of HuskyLens improved the color detection system. The current design provides a practical balance between mechanical simplicity, electronic functionality, and software control. The project will continue to be improved through further testing, especially in areas such as turning consistency and measured performance.

Our final objective remains to complete three laps within three minutes with the least number of accidents possible. Every part of the robot, from the lightweight LEGO chassis to the sensor system and Python program, contributes to this objective. The repository documents the development process and the current design so that the work completed by the team can be clearly understood and evaluated.
